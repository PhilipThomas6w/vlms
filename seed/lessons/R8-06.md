The Bible is made of 2 books, the Old Testament and the New Testament.

The Old Testament is about how the world began and the life of the Hebrew people [later on known as the Jewish people or Jews] and how God directs the people, punishes them and loves them. Throughout the writings [49 books] the coming of Jesus is foretold.

The New Testament has 27 books. The first 4 are the Gospels, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. These books tell us about the life of Jesus, his nativity, his childhood, ministry, crucifixion and resurrection. The rest of the books were chosen by devout men under the guidance of the Holy Spirit from several other books.

All the other books tell us about the Early Church, how the followers of Jesus spread the faith across the Mediterranean and beyond.

The next book after the Gospels is the Acts of the Apostles which tells the story of Pentecost, the coming of the Holy Spirit and how the church increased in number and the stories of St. Peter and St. Paul.

The first Apostles were the followers of Jesus, and mostly Jewish, who spread the Word, healed the sick and followed the teachings of Jesus. St. Paul in particular wrote his first 9 letters to churches to encourage them in their faith and sometimes to chastise them where there was wrong understanding. Jesus’ teaching meant that people needed to change their thinking and sometimes be different in their communities. These letters explained why they should love all people, show kindness and understanding, share what they had and most of all worship God.

St. Paul wrote letters to the new churches:

1. Romans {Rome} - General instruction to the church
2. First Corinthians {Corinth} - Corinth, prominent city but a centre of false religion. A letter of correction
3. Second Corinthians - is about the new church, repentance and authority
4. Galatians - to several churches in Asia Minor
5. Ephesians {Ephesus} - a clear directive to the church on how to conduct itself.
6. Philippians {Philippi} - the epistle of joy.
7. Colosssians {Colossae} - presents Christ as the head of the church
8. First Thessalonians {Thessalonica} - a letter of encouragement
9. Second Thessalonians - to stand fast to the teachings of the church

10. First Timothy - to an individual [by now Paul is getting very old]
11. Second Timothy - last letter written awaiting martyrdom encouraging Timothy in the faith.
12. Titus - advice from Paul as he goes to Crete to lead the church and becomes first bishop.
13. Philemon - is a Christian slave owner and Paul writes to him explaining how he should conduct himself.
14. Hebrews - last epistle - letter to Jewish believers but it *may* have had another author.

15. James - First bishop of Jerusalem writes to fellow Jews.

16. First Peter - Urges obedience to God and man
17. Second Peter - Discusses divine judgement.

18. First John [the Theologian] - is about God’s forgiveness, love for his children and eternal life.
19. Second John [the Theologian] - gives advice to a certain lady.
20. Third John [the Theologian] - commends believers Gaius and Demetrius.

21. Jude - the Lord’s brother writes to followers to keep the truth and finishes with a stunning benediction.

22. Revelations - [John the Theologian] - The Revelation of Jesus Christ. This speaks prophetically about judgement, salvation and the church.

These letters were copied faithfully, distributed to the churches and handed down until they were bound together to make the book of the New Testament.

4 Gospels + 1 Pentecost account + 22 Letters = The New Testament

### Languages of the New Testament

Jesus spoke in Aramaic. Aramaic is a family of languages which includes Hebrew, the language of the Jewish people.

The Romans spoke Latin.

The NT was written in Koine Greek - common language of the Eastern Mediterranean also spoken by Jews.

It was not academic so that ordinary people could understand.

### Dates

- 33AD - Crucifixion of Christ
- 70AD - Fall of Jerusalem
- 70AD, 80AD, 96AD, 150AD - Various suggested dates for the completion of The New Testament

## How the New Testament Came to Us

*Typed from a handout page.*

![Illustrated page “The New Testament” showing six stages: the first Christians gathered round a speaker under a tree, Paul’s letters being copied and passed on, an evangelist writing a Gospel at a desk, a runner carrying scrolls, and Church Fathers examining manuscripts at a table](how-the-new-testament-came-to-us.jpg)

There is much more evidence to go on when we come to the collection of the New Testament. Thousands of manuscripts (some going back to the early centuries AD) have been preserved and from them we can piece together what must have happened.

1. When the first Christian groups met together for worship, they continued to read the Jewish Old Testament, as Jesus had done. As well as this, people who had known Jesus were asked to talk about him and to share his teachings.
2. The first written documents circulating amongst the churches were the letters written by Paul. These were read and treasured by the people they were written to, and they were also copied and distributed to other churches nearby.
3. As time passed and eye-witnesses gradually died, the Christians realized that clear accounts of Jesus’ life and work must be written down. This led Matthew and John, Mark (working with Peter) and Doctor Luke (journeying with Paul), to write down their Gospels. As the early church grew, Luke added the Acts of the Apostles to his Gospel.
4. At first, there were many other accounts of Jesus’ life being circulated, but by the second century AD the leaders of the churches agreed that the four Gospels were the only genuinely inspired accounts.
5. John’s moving account of his visions about the end of time, called the Book of Revelation, was then added to the accepted documents. 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, James, Jude and Hebrews were also accepted, after some debate, probably about their suitability for reading aloud in church.
6. The list of books as we now have it was agreed and in use long before the Councils of Laodicea (AD 363) and of Carthage (AD 397) formally accepted them. In making their final decisions, the Church Fathers asked themselves two questions: ‘Does this book teach what the eye-witnesses, particularly the disciples of Jesus, taught?’ and ‘Does it encourage and strengthen Christians in their faith?’
