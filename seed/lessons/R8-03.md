## The Fall of Constantinople and the Church under Ottoman Rule

When the 4th Crusade attacked and took Constantinople in 1204 they fatally weakened the Byzantine Empire. They set up a Latin Empire in Constantinople but this was destroyed in 1261 when the Byzantine Emperor Michael VIII re-conquered Constantinople.

Unfortunately, the pressures from enemies surrounding the Empire particularly the Serbs, Latins in Greece and Turks in Asia Minor, became too great. The Empire became smaller and smaller and finally the Ottoman Turkish Sultan Mehmet II took and sacked Constantinople on Tuesday 29th May 1453. Many of the churches were turned into mosques and many of the inhabitants were made slaves. The last Emperor Constantine XI died on the walls defending his city. The Great Christian City was now in the hands of Moslem Turks.

The Orthodox Church continued however and Christians became a kind of state within a state with their own laws while paying double the taxes paid by the Moslems. There was pressure put on the Christians to become Moslems and some did this but then repented and were received back into the Church. If the Turks discovered that they had done this they were arrested, tortured and put to death, usually by hanging.

In 1821 the Greeks in Greece rose up against the Turks and a war started. The Greeks were given considerable help by Christian powers particularly by the Orthodox Russians and by the British Navy who destroyed the Turkish fleet at the battle of Navarino in 1827. Greece was formally recognised as independent by the Great Powers under the London Protocol in 1830.

In 1922 after a disastrous war between Turkey and Greece, there was an exchange of populations. Moslems in Greece had to leave Greece and go to Turkey, while Orthodox Christians in Turkey had to leave and go to Greece.

There are still Orthodox Christians in Constantinople however, and this is where the Ecumenical Patriarch is based to this day.

Turkey is now supposed to be a secular state.

## The Fall of Constantinople, 29th May 1453

*Typed from a handout page.*

In eastern Europe and the Near East two empires vied for power in the late Middle Ages. One was the remains of the Christian Byzantine empire. The other was the Muslim Ottoman empire, founded in about 1299 by the Turk Osman I.

The Ottoman Turks built up their empire quickly. From 1326 they proceeded to conquer large parts of the Byzantine empire. They controlled most of Greece, Bosnia, Albania and Bulgaria by 1450, and tried to conquer Hungary. All that was left of the Byzantine empire was Constantinople, which was founded on a village once called Byzantium.

In 1453 the Turks, under Mehmet II (Muhammad II), made a final assault on the city. The last Byzantine emperor, Constantine XI, had about 10,000 men under arms whereas Mehmet had between 100,000 and 150,000 soldiers.

![Photograph of the great domed church of St Sophia in Constantinople with a minaret on either side, seen over rooftops with the water beyond](st-sophia-with-minarets.jpg)

After Constantinople fell the Turks converted the Christian church of St Sophia into a mosque.

## The Walls of Constantinople

*Typed from handout pages.*

![Painting of the Theodosian walls of Constantinople seen from outside, with towers and battlements, and Turkish gunners firing a huge cannon from behind wooden screens and bundles of brushwood](theodosian-walls-bombarded.jpg)

**The Theodosian walls are bombarded by Mehmet the Conqueror, AD 1453.** Here we view the walls of Constantinople from the outside showing the bombardment by Mehmet the Conqueror in AD 1453. The Byzantine emperor had concentrated his troops between the inner and outer walls. It was the low-lying *mesoteichion* section of the walls across the Lycus Valley that was finally breached by the Turks.

![Painting of defenders and citizens on the terrace between the walls pointing up at a blue-robed figure of the Virgin Mary glowing above the outer wall, while arrows and a fireball fly towards a tall tower](peribolos-between-the-walls.jpg)

**The *peribolos* between the outer and inner walls, AD 1422.** The inner terrace between the inner and outer walls was called the *peribolos*, and accommodated the soldiers who defended the outer wall. It was between 50 and 64ft. wide. Beyond lay the outer wall, which was a modest structure compared with the inner wall. This plate shows a memorable moment during the Ottoman siege of AD 1422. An apparition of the Virgin Mary on the wall has heartened the citizens.
